Read the passage and answer the questions.

Dylan saved his allowance for weeks, and he finally bought a shiny robot toy. It looked strong in the store, and the box promised "Super Power Moves." Dylan felt proud, so he opened it right away. For three days he played with it after dinner, and he even showed it to his cousin. But on the fourth day, the robot's arm snapped when Dylan pressed a button. Dylan felt upset, and he wished he had checked the toy more carefully.

His mom said mistakes can teach smart habits. Dylan learned that ads and boxes can make products look better than they really are. Next time, he planned to look at the toy closely, and he would ask questions before paying. He also decided to compare prices and read simple reviews with an adult. Dylan still liked fun toys, but he wanted to spend money wisely. After that, he waited a day before buying something, so he could think calmly and choose well.

The Kim Family Budget Talk

Read the passage and answer the questions.

The Kim family met every Sunday afternoon to plan how to use their money. They called it their "family budget talk," and everyone had a turn to speak. Mr. Kim listed needs like groceries and bus cards. Mrs. Kim talked about saving for a winter trip. The children shared ideas too, but they had to explain why something mattered.

One week, the kids wanted to eat out, and they also wanted new art supplies. The family did not say yes right away. Instead, they checked their plan and talked calmly. They asked, "Do we need this now, or can it wait?" In the end, they chose the art supplies, and they cooked dinner at home. The kids felt proud because they helped make a fair choice.

Planning together taught the family patience and teamwork. It also helped them feel connected, because they listened to each other and made one plan. When families share decisions, they often build trust, and they learn how to spend with care.

The No-Spend Challenge

Read the passage and answer the questions.

One teacher gave her class a challenge: "Spend no money for one week." At first, the students worried, because buying snacks and toys felt normal. Still, they agreed to try, and they made a list of free ideas. Lily baked cookies at home instead of buying candy. Reggie invited friends over, and they played board games. Another group made paper airplanes and held a small contest.

After a few days, the students noticed something surprising. They laughed more, and they talked more, because they were sharing time instead of shopping. On Friday, the teacher asked them to reflect. Many students said they saved more money than they expected, but they also felt happier than before. They learned that fun does not always need a price tag.

The class also learned a new habit: pause before spending. When they wanted something, they asked, "Do I truly need this?" The challenge ended, but the lesson stayed. The students discovered that people and creativity can bring real joy.

Learning the Value of Money

Read the passage and answer the questions.

Money has value because people agree to use it to buy goods and services. Long ago, people traded things like eggs, rice, or cloth. Today, many people use coins, bills, or cards instead. Understanding the value of a dollar helps children see how time, effort, and choices connect.

Grace learned this when she earned five dollars by washing her neighbor's car. The work was not quick, but she kept going, and she finished carefully. Later, Grace spent the five dollars on ice cream for her family. She watched everyone smile, and she felt proud. Grace realized something important: money is not only for buying things for yourself. It can also be for sharing and creating happiness.

After that day, Grace respected money more. She asked herself, "How long did I work for this?" She also started making smaller plans, like saving part of her money and spending part. When children understand value, they make thoughtful choices, and they appreciate what they have.

Jason Chooses Carefully

Read the passage and answer the questions.

Money is limited, so people must choose what matters most. Jason learned this when he wanted two things at the same time: a new backpack and new shoes. His budget was small, and he could only buy one. Jason did not complain. Instead, he compared what he needed more. His old backpack was torn, and books almost fell out, so he chose the backpack.

After he bought it, Jason felt relieved, but he still wanted the shoes. His dad helped him make a simple plan. Jason wrote a goal: "Save for shoes." He also wrote small steps, like saving part of his allowance and not buying extra snacks. Jason learned that saying "no" now can mean saying "yes" later.

Choosing carefully taught Jason patience and planning. He also felt proud because he made a smart decision by himself. When people understand limits, they can set goals, stay balanced, and avoid wasting money. Jason's choice was not perfect, but it was thoughtful, and it helped him feel ready for the future.
